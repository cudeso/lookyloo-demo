After a summer of outages, Europe's web shops start watching themselves around the clock

Small retailers that once learned about downtime from angry customers are now paying for monitoring that checks their sites every 30 seconds.

By Ilse Marchetti, technology correspondent · 20 October 2026, 07:30 CET

When the checkout page of an Utrecht outdoor clothing shop stopped responding at 02:14 one night in September, nobody at the company noticed. The site was back 13 minutes later. The owner found out the next morning, from a monitoring report.

"Two years ago we would only have heard about it from a customer complaint, if at all," said the shop's operations manager, who asked not to be named because the company has not disclosed the incident to customers. "Now I get a message on my phone before anyone has time to complain."

The shop is one of a growing number of small European retailers buying website monitoring, a service that until recently was mostly used by banks and large platforms. Providers send automated requests to a customer's site from several cities, usually every 30 to 60 seconds, and raise an alert when the site stops answering or slows down.

Cheaper than a lost Saturday

Prices have fallen sharply. Entry-level plans now start at around €30 a month, compared with several hundred euros five years ago. Vendors say demand rose after a series of high-profile outages this summer, when a content delivery network fault and two cloud incidents took thousands of European web shops offline for hours.

"For a shop that does most of its business at the weekend, one lost Saturday afternoon costs more than a year of monitoring," said Clara Ostendorf, who covers retail technology for this newspaper.

"An alert at 02:15 is worth more than a perfect uptime percentage at the end of the month."

Most services also check TLS certificates and warn customers before they expire. According to an industry survey published last week, one in six unplanned outages last year was caused by an expired certificate.

Reports for the boardroom

Monitoring companies increasingly sell monthly availability reports alongside alerts. They combine uptime figures, response time charts and a short explanation of each incident, written for managers rather than engineers.

Not everyone is convinced. A Brussels consultancy that advises small businesses on IT spending warned that monitoring only reports problems and does not fix them. "If nobody is on call when the alert arrives, you have simply paid to be told bad news faster," it said.

The Utrecht shop has since added an automatic health check to its deployment process. It has not had an outage since.
