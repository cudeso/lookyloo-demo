How a picture of a shield keeps illicit marketplaces trading

Researchers say badges, escrow and public feedback do more to hold criminal forums together than any technology, and that the same habits leave traces investigators can follow.

By Tomasz Wierzbicki, security correspondent · 19 October 2026, 18:00 CET

Underground forums have a trust problem. Their members use aliases, deal with strangers and cannot go to court when a trade goes wrong. Yet some of these marketplaces run for years, with tens of thousands of members and steady trade.

According to a study by the Halvard Institute, an independent research group in Ghent, the answer is mostly social rather than technical. Over 18 months, its researchers followed the public sections of eleven forums and found that reputation systems did most of the work.

Badges, bonds and feedback

The most visible signal is a small badge next to a vendor's name. On many forums it means staff have reviewed the vendor and hold a deposit, often called a bond, that can be paid out to buyers if the vendor disappears.

"The badge itself is just an image file," said Dr Liesbeth Vermeulen, who led the study. "What gives it value is everything around it: the bond, the dispute section, the feedback threads. When members stop believing in those, the forum collapses quickly."

"The badge itself is just an image file. What gives it value is everything around it."

That collapse happened last week to Cobalt Den, a marketplace whose administrators disappeared with users' escrow deposits. Members of rival forums were openly debating whether escrow could still be trusted within hours.

Traces in the code

The same habits that make forums work also leave traces. Forum operators often reuse templates, stylesheets and images across several websites, including ones that look unrelated, such as hosting shops or service companies.

Investigators can compare those files across sites. When two websites serve an identical file, its hash matches even if its name and location differ. Vermeulen cautioned that such a match is only a starting point: many legitimate sites share open-source libraries and stock images. "A shared file tells you where to look next. It does not tell you who is behind a site."

The Halvard Institute plans to publish its dataset, with all usernames removed, early next year.
